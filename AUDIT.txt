COMBAT WOMBAT - FULL AUDIT
==========================
2026-10-16. Covers the live build (main): physics, game feel, game design,
art and sound, and the code, then answers "would an engine have been better,
and does it need rebuilding?"

How this was done: reading every system in the code, plus the simulation
runs from the last few slices. I can't hold a phone and play it, so the
"feel" findings are traced to the code that causes them, and they should be
checked against how it feels in your hands.

Severity: [!!!] hurts the core of the game  [!!] clearly noticeable  [!] polish


0. THE SHORT ANSWER
-------------------

You're right that the physics feel clunky, and the reason is specific: the
game doesn't really simulate a tower. Poops are frozen in place the moment
they land, the "wobble" is a single number drawn as a lean, and the platform
can move at any speed without the tower noticing. Most of the clunk you
feel comes from rules written to paper over that (see section 1).

That is a design choice in the physics, not the lack of a game engine. An
engine would hand you a real physics simulation, but so can a small physics
library dropped into this codebase. My recommendation (section 7) is:

  - Don't rebuild everything from scratch in a big engine. Most of what's
    here (art, sound, saves, campaign, Workshop, stickers, offline play,
    the test harness) works and would have to be rebuilt for little gain.
  - Do rebuild the stacking core on a real rigid-body physics library
    (Planck.js or Rapier), prototyped first as a separate page so you can
    play the old and new side by side before committing.
  - Then trim the design: the game has grown many systems in a short time
    while the core stacking feel hasn't changed since the first sprint.

If you want to grow far past what's here (lots of animated characters,
story scenes, consoles or app stores), section 6 covers when a full engine
starts to pay off and which one.


1. PHYSICS
----------

How it works now (shared/physics.js):
  - Falling poops are axis-aligned boxes with gravity and hand-written
    collision rules.
  - When a poop comes to rest it is "settled": frozen into the pile at a
    fixed offset from the platform centre. Settled poops never move again
    (unless diarrhea melts what's under them).
  - The wobble is one spring value (pileSway) driven by how far the pile's
    centre of mass sits outside the middle 72% of the platform, plus a
    sine wave for "nervousness".
  - The tower is drawn sheared sideways by that value. When the leaning
    centre of mass passes the platform edge plus a margin, the whole tower
    falls as a scripted animation and the run (or level) ends.

[!!!] 1.1 Moving the platform has no effect on the tower
      steerPlatform (shared/core.js) moves the platform up to 9 px a frame
      (540 px/s), or snaps 35% of the way to your finger every frame. The
      pile is stored as offsets from the platform, so it rides along glued
      on. Nothing feeds platform speed or acceleration into the wobble.
      This removes the most natural skill in a balancing game (carry the
      tower carefully, don't jerk it) and makes the platform feel weightless.

[!!!] 1.2 The tower you see isn't where the physics thinks it is
      drawStack (shared/art.js) shears the tower sideways by
      sway x height x 0.35, but collisions use the un-sheared positions.
      With the drawn sway at its limit (0.5), a poop 300 px up the tower is
      drawn 52 px from where the game treats it as being. So when the tower
      leans visibly, a new poop can land on thin air next to the drawn top,
      or pass into its side. This is probably a large part of the "clunky"
      feeling.

[!!!] 1.3 Settled poops never react to anything
      A poop resting half off another one doesn't tip; a tall thin stack
      doesn't buckle; a big poop landing hard doesn't jostle the ones below.
      The only failure is the all-or-nothing topple. There are no near-misses
      ("it slid but held"), no partial collapses, and no recovering by moving
      under a slipping poop. That's where most of the drama in stacking
      games (Tricky Towers, Jenga) comes from.

[!!] 1.4 Corner cases are handled by teleporting
      Several rules move poops by hand when the box collision can't decide:
        - a poop overhanging too far is shoved 2.2-7 px sideways each frame
          until it falls (stepPlops, "shove it off decisively")
        - a poop squeezed between two others snaps to the gap's centre and
          sits on top (collideFallingPoop, "squeezed from both sides")
        - a poop in contact for 40 frames without settling is parked on top
          of whatever is under its centre (stepPlops, p.contact > 40)
        - grazing a corner shoves it off rather than letting it tip
      Each one is reasonable on its own, but together they read as poops
      jumping, snapping and sliding in ways real objects don't.

[!!] 1.5 Rotation is only for looks
      Falling poops spin, and settled ones can sit tilted on a bridge, but
      every collision treats them as upright boxes. A tilted poop's drawn
      corner can overlap a neighbour, or float above its support.

[!!] 1.6 The wobble meter moves on its own
      The nervous sway adds a sine wave scaled by how precarious the pile
      is. The campaign also gives the pile random kicks when a Stomper
      lands. To the player this looks like the meter twitching for no
      reason they can see or influence.

[!!] 1.7 Topple is a threshold, then a cutscene
      Once the leaning centre of mass crosses the edge, every poop falls
      straight down with a little scatter. There's no moment where you
      see it going and can save it (except the Anchor and Slow-Mo
      upgrades, which exist to patch exactly this).

[!] 1.8 Two different physics tunings
      Endless and the campaign use different sway, damping and fall speeds
      (the TUNE tables). A player who learns one mode has to relearn the
      other's feel.

[!] 1.9 What's good and worth keeping
      - The fixed 60-steps-a-second clock: the game plays the same at
        60/120/144 Hz.
      - Full determinism, so tools/compare.js can prove a change didn't
        alter play. That's rare and valuable.
      - Melting and the "collapsed" re-falls are a fun idea that real
        physics would make even better.


2. CONTROLS AND GAME FEEL
-------------------------

[!!] 2.1 The poop source is hard to read
      The wombat changes direction at random (a 0.8% chance every frame)
      and poops on a random timer, so where the next poop drops is
      mostly luck. Steady Walker, Centre Line, Telegraph and the Lookout
      mate all exist to patch this. A readable, rhythmic wombat (a
      visible walk-and-squat pattern you can learn) would make catching
      a skill, not a reaction test.

[!!] 2.2 No preview of what's coming
      Poop size (30-78 px) is the biggest factor in stacking well, and you
      learn it only when it appears. Stacking games usually show the next
      piece. A "next poop" bubble over the wombat would add planning.

[!!] 2.3 Pointer steering is lag-then-snap
      With a finger, the platform closes 35% of the gap every frame. That
      is snappy on small moves and slightly floaty on big ones, with no
      sense of weight. Keyboard is a constant 9 px a frame with no
      acceleration. Once the tower reacts to motion (1.1), both need a
      proper acceleration curve.

[!] 2.4 Feedback is good on catches, weak on danger
      Landing a poop has a squish, stars, a sound and a little shake. But
      the moment that matters most (the tower starting to go) has only
      the meter and the lean. A creak sound exists, but danger has no
      dedicated camera move, slow-down or colour cue.

[!] 2.5 Screen shake on every landing
      A shake on every settle (+3, up to 10) adds up during busy stretches.
      Shake is best saved for big moments.


3. GAME DESIGN
--------------

Endless mode

[!!] 3.1 The core loop has one decision
      Catch the poop, put it near the middle. Since you can't drop or
      reposition a poop without losing toilet paper, there's little choice
      about where to put a poop or when to take a risk. Ideas with real
      choice: aim for score zones on the tower, golden poops that only
      count if placed on top, a "flick" to reject a bad poop at some cost,
      or combos for matching sizes.

[!!] 3.2 Runs end abruptly and all at once
      Three misses or one topple ends the run. Topples come from a slow
      build-up the player can't see well (1.2, 1.6), so a run can end
      without the player understanding why.

[!] 3.3 Difficulty is pace-only
      Endless gets harder by shortening the gap between poops. Levels
      look and play the same. A new hazard, poop type or wind per level
      would make climbing feel like progress.

The campaign

[!!!] 3.4 Lots of systems on top of an unchanged core
      In about two weeks, the game gained:
        - 7 rival personalities and 12 mate perks
        - 8 buffs and debuffs
        - 9 upgrade trees (5 platform, 4 wombat)
        - seasons, Gum Nuts, 38 looks and 32 stickers
      Most of these change numbers (+15 px, x0.85 steering, -30%
      diarrhea), and most only matter because the core is fiddly. Many
      can't be felt at all: Fibre Diet, Regular and Steady Walker change
      odds the player never sees. Meanwhile the stacking itself plays the
      same in a season-5 level as in the first minute.

[!!] 3.5 Every level is the same goal
      Every campaign level is "finish N stacks to the line", whether it's a
      mate or a rival, with different numbers. Mates and rivals could ask
      for different things: a mate wants a pretty (wobble-free) tower; a
      rival dares you to build tall fast; a boss wants you to survive.

[!!] 3.6 The map is a timer more than a place
      Rivals march in on a clock and mates wander off on one. That creates
      pressure, but choosing whom to visit comes down to arithmetic. The
      Sage mate exists to show the numbers. Fewer, more distinct nodes
      could make the choice about what kind of level you want.

[!] 3.7 Balance hasn't met a human yet
      Prices, charm goals and rival speeds were set by simulated bots. The
      playtest and balance phases in RELEASE-PLAN.txt never happened, and
      the game is now much bigger than the plan assumed.

[!] 3.8 Lots of text
      The campaign has 19 different toast messages, plus chips, banners and
      a picker. Much of the game is explained in sentences scrolling past
      while you're trying to catch poops.

Progression

[!] 3.9 The two economies overlap
      Upgrades, looks and stickers are three reward tracks with one
      currency, which is fine, but upgrades make the game easier at
      exactly the point it should be getting more interesting. Consider
      upgrades that change how you play (Springs, Catapult, Mega Cube are
      good examples) over plain number bumps.

What's strong

  - The premise is funny and memorable, and the tone is consistent.
  - The Bush wombat design reads well and has character.
  - Mates on the plank and rivals bullying you from it are genuinely
    good ideas. They put the meta-game into the stacking screen.
  - The front page, offline play and the "no build step, opens anywhere"
    setup are lean and work well.


4. ART, ANIMATION AND SOUND
---------------------------

[!!] 4.1 Animation is all procedural, and it shows
      Everything is drawn in code each frame (shared/art.js). That gave a
      clean, consistent outline style cheaply, but motion is limited to
      bobbing, sine-wave wiggles and a squish factor. No anticipation and
      follow-through on the squat, no real walk cycle, no faces reacting
      to the tower. The wombat is the star, and it's the least animated
      thing in a good-looking scene.

[!!] 4.2 The layout is cramped at the top
      The top plank sits 132 px from the top of a 960 px screen. The
      wombat's head nearly touches the edge; hats get cut off; the HUD
      panels in endless overlap it. Endless and the campaign also put their
      HUDs in different places (top vs bottom).

[!] 4.3 Emoji carry a lot of the UI
      Icons for buffs, perks, rivals, stickers and buttons are emoji. They
      look different on every phone (and blank on some older ones), which
      clashes with the hand-drawn outline art. RELEASE-PLAN.txt flagged
      this (I7) and it was never checked.

[!] 4.4 Backgrounds are plain
      A flat sky gradient, clouds and a grass strip. The campaign map is a
      ring of nodes on green. Habitats (NEXT-IDEAS 3A) would help most.

[!] 4.5 Sound is functional but thin
      16 synthesised effects, no music. The sounds are short and similar
      in tone. Music and a richer poop "plop" palette would add a lot.

What's strong: the outline style is consistent across wombats, poops,
platforms and UI, the colours read clearly, and Comic Neue suits the tone.
The cosmetics show the style can take on more variety well.


5. CODE AND TECH
----------------

[!!] 5.1 Big single-file pages
      wombat-campaign.html is 1,326 lines in one script: map, levels,
      rivals, mates, picker, saves and all the drawing glue. Adding
      features is getting slower and riskier.

[!!] 5.2 UI is hand-built on the canvas
      Buttons, the picker, the pause menu and banners are drawn and
      hit-tested by hand (17 hit-test calls across the two modes, each with
      hard-coded rectangles). This is where an engine or UI library saves
      the most time, and where accessibility suffers most (screen readers
      and keyboard focus can't see canvas buttons).

[!] 5.3 No unit tests, but an excellent equivalence harness
      tools/compare.js is a strong safety net for "did this refactor
      change play?". It can't tell you whether play is good. The ad-hoc
      season, mate and outfit harnesses live outside the repo.

[!] 5.4 Fine as is
      - Load time and performance: about 5,000 lines total, no
        dependencies, works offline and from file://.
      - Saves: versioned and repaired on load.
      - The service worker and version stamping work.


6. WOULD AN ENGINE HAVE BEEN BETTER?
------------------------------------

What an engine gives you, and whether this game needs it:

  Real physics (rigid bodies, friction, tipping, joints)
      Needed: YES. This is the big one, but a physics library gives it
      without an engine (see 7).
  Animation tools (sprite sheets, skeletal rigs, tweening, timelines)
      Would help: YES, for the wombat (4.1).
  Scene, UI and layout tools
      Would help: YES, the hand-made canvas UI is costly (5.2).
  Particles, cameras, screen effects
      Would help: somewhat. The current ones are simple but fine.
  Audio mixing, music
      Would help: somewhat.
  Export to app stores and consoles
      Only if you want that.
  Save systems, input, scaling
      No. Already solved here.

The engine options, honestly:

  Phaser (JavaScript, web-first)
      + Stays a web game. Built-in scenes, input, tweens, cameras,
        particles, audio, scaling, plus Matter.js physics.
      + Your JavaScript skills and much of the logic carry over.
      - Matter.js is known for jittery tall stacks, the exact case here.
        Use Planck.js or Rapier with Phaser instead.
      - The drawn-in-code art would need redoing as sprites to get the
        most out of it (which is also the fix for 4.1).
  Godot 4 (free, open source)
      + A real editor for scenes, animation and UI; good 2D tools; exports
        to desktop, mobile and web.
      - Web builds are several megabytes and load noticeably slower,
        especially on phones. Today's game is tiny and instant.
      - Everything would be rewritten in GDScript, from scratch.
      - Its built-in 2D physics is OK at stacking, not great.
  Unity
      - Heavy for a small 2D web game; slow web loading; licence history.
        Not a good fit here.

Verdict: if this had started in Phaser (or Godot), the game would probably
have real physics and better animation today, because those would have been
the easy path. But the clunky physics comes from one modelling decision
("freeze poops, fake the wobble"), and that can be fixed here without
throwing away the rest.


7. RECOMMENDED PATH
-------------------

Step 1  Physics prototype, side by side (a few days)
        A standalone page, physics-lab.html, using a real 2D physics
        library:
          - Planck.js: a Box2D port, pure JavaScript, deterministic, good
            at stacking.
          - Rapier: WebAssembly, very stable stacks, with a deterministic
            build.
        Poops are real boxes with friction and a little rounding; the
        platform is a moving body that carries them with inertia; the
        wombat drops poops exactly as now. No campaign, no upgrades: just
        "is stacking fun now?". Tune friction, mass, damping and sleeping
        until tall stacks are stable but nervous, then play it against the
        current endless on your phone.

        Watch-outs: rigid-body stacks on a moving platform can feel too
        slippery or too twitchy. Tricky Towers shows it can be great, but
        it takes tuning time, and it's the step to decide on before
        anything else.

Step 2  If the prototype wins: swap the stacking core
        Replace stepPlops / updateStack / the fake wobble with the physics
        world, behind the same S (run) object, so the modes, HUD, saves,
        campaign and Workshop keep working. Rework the physics-based
        upgrades as real physics (Sticky Pad = higher friction, Heavy Base
        = heavier bottom poops, Springs = restitution). Topple becomes
        "poops fell off", with partial losses instead of instant game over.
        The determinism harness still works: both libraries are
        deterministic in the same browser.

Step 3  Design trim and focus (alongside step 2)
        - Make the wombat readable (rhythm, telegraphed squat) and show
          the next poop.
        - Give mate and rival levels different goals.
        - Cut or merge the upgrades the player can't feel.
        - One HUD layout for both modes, with less text.

Step 4  Presentation
        - Keyframed wombat animation (squat, strain, relief, celebrate,
          watch the tower nervously).
        - More room at the top of the screen.
        - Habitats.
        - Music.
        - Replace emoji icons with drawn ones.

Step 5  Only if you want app stores, consoles or a much bigger game
        Port to Godot, or to Phaser for web-plus-wrappers, using the tuned
        physics and design from steps 1-4 as the spec. A rebuild then
        copies a game you know is fun, instead of hoping an engine makes
        it fun.

The one thing I'd do first: Step 1. It answers your question with your own
hands instead of my reading of the code, and it's cheap to throw away if the
current feel turns out to be the better game.
